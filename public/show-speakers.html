When you ask a computer to show my speakers, you really want two answers: which of the types of speakers you own, and whether each one still plays sound. This guide lets you test your setup first, then explains how every loudspeaker turns an audio signal into the music you hear.

Show My Speakers: Test the Speakers You Already Have

Not sure your speakers are connected and working? A quick sound test answers that in under a minute, right in the browser. Your computer sends an audio signal to a playback device, and the operating system decides which playback device receives it, so the first job is confirming that the right one is selected. A laptop might quietly send sound to a monitor, a pair of Bluetooth headphones or the built-in speaker instead of the pair on your desk. Check this before you blame your whole home audio setup, or assume the audio system itself is broken. Run a bass test to check whether your speakers handle sub-bass or roll off above 60Hz.

Run a sound test on each speaker channel

Play a short tone through the left speaker, then through the right speaker. If the tone arrives from the correct side each time, your stereo speakers are wired the right way round and the left channel and right channel are both alive. Turn the volume all the way up for this check only, because a speaker that sounds dead may simply be playing very quietly. Then bring the volume back down before you play music. Your hearing varies from day to day, so repeat the check once before you decide a speaker is faulty.

What to do if your speakers give no sound

Work through the cheap fixes first, since most failures are loose cables rather than broken hardware:

  • Make sure the speakers are powered on and plugged into electricity.
  • Check that the speaker cable runs to the green jack on the back of a desktop, or to the headphone socket on a laptop.
  • Look at the volume in your operating system and on any physical knob.
  • Confirm that the correct playback device is enabled, because the system may be sending the tone to a different jack.

If nothing helps, the audio driver may be at fault. Search for the latest audio driver for your sound card from its maker, install it, restart and run the test again. A missing or outdated audio driver is the most common reason a healthy speaker system stays silent after an update.

Fix stereo speakers that only play one channel

When only one side makes noise, the two speakers are often not connected to each other. In many desktop designs a single wire runs from the computer to the main unit, and you must link the second speaker to the first yourself. Swap the left and right plugs to find out whether the cable or the speaker is the problem, because the dead side follows the faulty part.

Types of Speakers You Can Have at Home

Every home has its own mix of home speakers, and each type of speakers is built for a particular job: some favour music, some favour movies, and some favour the garden. Knowing the main types of speakers helps you recognise what you already own and decide which one to add next. The table sums up the usual fit for each, so choose by the sound you want, not by the badge.

Floor-Standing Speakers

Floor-standing speakers, also called tower speakers, are tall cabinets that hold several drivers and a large enclosure, often called the speaker cabinet. They reproduce deep bass and clear vocals with little help, which makes them a favourite when you want a complete sound system for a living room.

Bookshelf Speakers

Bookshelf speakers are compact two-way designs that fit on a shelf, a desk or a stand. They suit smaller rooms and give detailed sound without eating floor space, and many listeners count them among the best speakers for a first stereo pair.

In-Wall and In-Ceiling Speakers

In-wall speakers and in-ceiling speakers disappear into the room, so the only thing you notice is the sound. They are popular for home cinema because they create surround sound without cabinets on the floor, and an angled tweeter helps aim the sound at the seats. Aesthetics are the main reason people choose them.

Soundbars for TV Sound

A soundbar is a long, slim cabinet with several drivers that sits under or above your TV. It packs the left, centre and right speakers into one box, so dialogue is easy to follow and setup takes minutes. Soundbars are a smart pick when the room is too tight for separate speakers.

Subwoofers for Deep Bass

A subwoofer handles only the lowest notes, the rumble in an action film or the kick drum in a dance track. Because low bass is hard to locate by ear, a single subwoofer can sit in a corner and still feel natural. Most good speaker systems pair one subwoofer with smaller speakers, and some larger rooms use two subwoofers to even out the bass.

Outdoor Speakers

Outdoor speakers are sealed against rain, sun and wind, so you can play music at a barbecue or in the garden without worry. They trade a little sound quality for durability, and they often need a mount rather than a stand.

Surround Speakers

Surround speakers sit beside or behind the listening position and create the sense of being inside the scene, which matters for a home theater and for games. They are the last speakers most people add to a home cinema, after the front three.

Wireless vs. Wired Speakers and Active vs. Passive Speaker Systems

Two choices shape every purchase: how audio reaches the speaker, and where the amplifier lives. Wireless models stream over Bluetooth or Wi-Fi and keep a room tidy, while wired speakers use a cable and usually offer steadier, higher-quality sound. A cable never drops out, which is why many hi-fi owners stay wired for their main system.

Active Speakers

Active speakers have a built-in amplifier, so you connect a source straight to them. They are simple, compact and ideal for a desk or a small apartment, and they save you from matching an external receiver.

Passive Speakers

Passive speakers contain no amplifier. They need an external amplifier or receiver, which gives you freedom to upgrade each part separately and to chase high fidelity over time. The trade-off is a little more wiring and a little more planning.

Wireless Speakers

Wireless speakers still need power, and most also carry a built-in amplifier, so a wireless speaker is usually an active design with a radio inside. A premium wireless loudspeaker can match a wired pair for sound, but its batteries and network connection add things that can fail.

Checking a Bookshelf Speaker System Against a 45-Watt Amplifier

I'm helping a friend work out whether the speakers she already has, a bookshelf pair on her desk shelf, can fill a room properly, or whether she needs a bigger amplifier. The decision: keep the 45-watt amplifier she owns, or spend money on a stronger one. The spec sheet gives 87.5 dB sensitivity at 1 W and 1 m, 8 ohms, and her chair sits 2.6 m from each loudspeaker.

I run the speaker specifications through the level formula in this order: start with sensitivity, add 10 log10(45) = 16.5 dB for the power, then subtract 20 log10(2.6) = 8.3 dB for distance. That gives 87.5 + 16.5 − 8.3 = 95.7 dB peak from one speaker. A recomputation by hand with a calculator agrees to 0.1 dB.

The reference point is 85 dB, the average level most film mixers calibrate to, with peaks well above it. Reaching 85 dB on average needs only 3.8 W, so everyday listening is easy. The trouble is headroom: 95.7 dB leaves just 10.7 dB above that average, and loud passages in a film or a dense mix can need 15 dB, which is about 120 W at this distance.

Change one inputPeak level
Original: 87.5 dB, 2.6 m, 45 W95.7 dB
Move the chair to 2.0 m98.0 dB

What stands out is that distance is the cheapest upgrade: moving her chair closer buys 2.3 dB, as much as a 70 percent bigger amplifier. The input to watch is sensitivity, because a 3.5 dB gap needs more than double the power to close. One caveat: a rating quoted at 2.83 V rather than 1 W reads roughly 3 dB higher on a 4-ohm speaker, so check the fine print before comparing two sheets. My call is to keep the amplifier and move the chair, then revisit the speaker system only if clipping appears at her normal volume.

Inside a Loudspeaker: How Speaker Drivers Make Sound

A loudspeaker, often just called a speaker, combines one or more drivers, an enclosure and the electrical connections, sometimes with a crossover network. The driver is an electroacoustic part that converts an electrical audio signal into sound waves by moving air. The dynamic loudspeaker was invented in 1925 by Edward Kellogg and Chester Rice, and almost every loudspeaker made today still follows their moving-coil idea. Each of these speaker drivers has a job tied to a range of pitch, and a driver reproduces a band of frequencies best when its size matches the wavelength it must move.

That relationship is simple: λ = c / f where c is the speed of sound, about 343 m/s in air, and f is the frequency. A 20 Hz bass note stretches about 17 m, while a 10 kHz note is only 3.4 cm, which is why one cone cannot do everything well.

Think of a three-way loudspeaker as a team. The drivers share the audio, each driver stays inside its comfort zone, and the sound that reaches you is the sum of every driver working together. A single driver pushed beyond its range adds distortion, so a well-designed loudspeaker keeps each driver away from notes it cannot reproduce cleanly. Larger drivers move more air for bass, smaller drivers react faster for treble, and the match between drivers decides how natural the sound feels in your room. When you compare speaker systems, count the drivers and check how the maker divides the work between them, because more drivers do not always mean better sound.

Voice Coil, Diaphragm and Magnet

When the audio signal passes through the voice coil, a coil of wire sitting in the field of a permanent magnet, the coil moves back and forth. The coil is attached to the diaphragm, usually shaped as a speaker cone for strength, and the diaphragm pushes the air to make sound waves. A suspension keeps the voice coil centred, and a basket holds the whole assembly rigid. Because a loudspeaker works like a microphone in reverse, the same moving-coil design can serve in both.

Full-Range Drivers and Component Speakers

A full-range driver tries to cover the whole audible band with a single diaphragm. A full-range design is cheap, simple and phase-coherent, but a single cone struggles at the extremes. Component speakers split the job: the woofers and tweeters are separate and each is tuned for its own range, which usually sounds fuller. Many car doors use a full-range pair, while an upgrade often means moving to component speakers.

Woofer, Mid-Range Driver and Tweeter

A woofer is the large driver for low frequencies, and a subwoofer goes lower still. A mid-range driver covers the voice region, and the tweeter is the small, light driver for the highest notes. In a two-way loudspeaker one woofer and one tweeter share the work, while a three-way loudspeaker adds a mid-range driver. A coaxial design places the tweeter in the centre of the woofer to save space. Treble from the tweeters sets how airy a recording feels, and the woofers deliver most of the bass.

Crossover Networks and Enclosures

The crossover inside each loudspeaker is a filter that sends each driver only the frequencies it handles well, so the tweeter never receives heavy bass. The enclosure matters just as much: without a cabinet, sound from the rear of the cone cancels the sound from the front. A sealed enclosure gives tight bass, while a bass reflex design adds a tuned port that extends it. A solid enclosure also stops panels from rattling and keeps the loudspeaker free of distortion.

Speaker Wiring Connections

Most passive models use binding posts that accept bare speaker wire or banana plugs. Use a gauge of speaker cable thick enough for the run, and keep both loudspeaker leads the same length so the left speaker and right speaker see the same resistance.

Reading Your Speakers' Specifications

The numbers on a speaker box look intimidating, but three of them predict most of what you will hear. They tell you how loud a loudspeaker plays, how much it needs from your amplifier and whether the pair will produce balanced sound in your room. Start with sensitivity, because it sets how hard your amplifier must work. Run the headphone test whenever audio sounds off-balance or one side seems quieter.

Sensitivity and Efficiency

Sensitivity states how loud a driver plays at one metre with one watt, measured in decibels. A higher figure means the speaker plays louder from the same power, so a 92 dB model needs far less amplifier effort than an 84 dB model. Efficiency is the same idea as a percentage, and the two are related but not identical. Because decibels use logarithms, the change between two power levels follows Δ L = 10log₁₀(P_2 / P_1) so doubling a 50-watt amplifier to 100 watts adds only 3 dB.

Impedance and Electrical Characteristics

Impedance, measured in ohms, is the load the loudspeaker presents to the amplifier. Most home models are rated at 8 ohms, and some at 4 ohms, which asks the amplifier for more current. Impedance rises and falls across the frequency range, so a nominal rating is only a guide. Match it to your amplifier or receiver and the electrical side takes care of itself.

Power Handling in Watts

Power handling is how much power a speaker takes before the voice coil overheats or the cone is damaged, rated in watts. Pick speakers whose rating sits above your amplifier output and you avoid the most common cause of burnt drivers, which is clipping from an underpowered amplifier played too loud and distorting the sound.

Choosing the Right Speakers for Your Space

The right speakers are the ones that suit your room, your budget and what you play. A beautiful pair in the wrong space still sounds poor, so think about the room before the brand.

Room Size and Speaker Placement

Room size sets how much bass you can use: a big space needs more output, while a small one pushes bookshelf pairs toward the wall and makes bass boomy. Placement matters too. Corners boost bass response, which can turn a small room muddy. Keep each speaker away from corners, aim the tweeters at ear height and form a rough triangle with your seat to hold a steady soundstage across the whole speaker system. When the listening environment is lively, soft furnishings tame reflections.

What Will You Use Them For?

Music lovers should look for balanced, detailed bookshelf or tower speakers. Movie fans should start from the center channel, because it carries most of the dialogue and a large share of the soundtrack. Pick front speakers that match its tone for timbre matching, so panning across the front sounds smooth, and use surround speakers that are close in tone. Good imaging depends on that match more than on price.

Car Speakers and Factory Speaker Upgrades

For car speakers, first check what fits your vehicle, since door and dash openings dictate the speaker size. A voice-matched set from the same brand and series keeps front and rear consistent. Replacing the factory speakers is among the cheapest ways to improve car audio, and rear speakers matter less if you rarely carry passengers. If the budget is tight, upgrade the front pair first, then buy new speakers for the rear later.

Speaker Details FAQ — Frequently Asked Questions

Why can't I see all my connected speakers in the list?

Browser audio device enumeration requires microphone permission — the browser uses mic permission as a gate to prevent fingerprinting via device enumeration. If you denied microphone access previously, the device list may be incomplete or show only the default output. Grant microphone access when prompted (even if you have no microphone) and refresh the page. Also, some audio devices register only as input devices and will not appear in the speaker output list.

What does "Default Device" mean in my speaker details?

The default device is the audio output endpoint that applications use unless they specify a different one. When you play a YouTube video or join a Zoom call without selecting a specific output, the audio goes to the default device. You can change the default in OS sound settings (Windows: Sound Control Panel → Playback tab; macOS: System Settings → Sound → Output). The speaker detail table shows whether a device is currently set as your default.

Why does my speaker show as "disconnected" even though I can hear audio?

Some browsers cannot always determine the live connection status of audio endpoints, particularly for HDMI outputs and Bluetooth devices. The status shown is reported by the OS driver, which may lag behind the actual hardware state. If audio is playing through the device, it is functionally connected regardless of what the status label says. Use the built-in speaker test in this tool to confirm audio output rather than relying solely on the status indicator.

Can I see the model name and specifications of my speakers through the browser?

The browser can only access what the OS audio driver exposes — typically the device name, device ID, group ID, sample rate, and channel count. Detailed hardware specifications like driver size, power rating, or frequency response are not available through any browser API. For full hardware details on a specific speaker model, check the manufacturer's product page using the model name shown in the device list.
