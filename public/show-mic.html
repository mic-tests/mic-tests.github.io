Wondering what is plugged into your desk? This guide helps you show my mic details by walking through the label, connector and specifications that identify any microphone, so you can match your equipment to the types of microphones used for a podcast, a presentation or home recording. By the end you will know how to tell a dynamic mic from a condenser, read the sound pattern it picks up and decide whether it suits your audio needs.

Show My Mic: Find Out What Microphone You Have

Start with the obvious clues. Pick up your mic and look for a brand and model printed on the body, the base or the cable collar. A model name such as SM58 or AT2020 lets you look up the full spec sheet in seconds. If nothing is printed, check the sound settings on your computer: the operating system lists every audio input by name, and a USB device usually reports its own model there. Once you know the exact microphone, every later section of this guide becomes easy to apply to your own setup, whether you are chasing clean sound for recording or just a clearer voice on calls. Record your voice directly in the browser with the online mic recorder and download as MP3 or WAV.

Check Your Built-in Microphone First

Many people already own a built-in microphone without realizing it. Laptops, cell phones, action cameras and mirrorless camera bodies all carry small built-in mics that convert sound to a signal for the headphones, the call app or the memory card. They work if that is all you have, but keep them about an arm's length away, since they offer little shielding against wind noise.

Check the XLR or USB Connector: Wired or Wireless

The connector tells you a great deal. A three-pin XLR plug points to a studio or stage mic that needs an interface or mixer, and often phantom power if it is a condenser. A USB plug means the mic carries its own converter and works straight from a computer. Look for a wired cable or a small wireless receiver, and a battery door that signals a transmitter. The round metal grille at the top hides the capsule, and its shape helps too: the ball-shaped head is why the microphone emoji looks the way it does. If you keep a whole microphone cabinet of gear, label each mic once identified so you never have to guess again.

How Microphones Work: From Sound Waves to Electric Signals

A microphone is a transducer: it turns the pressure of sound waves into electric signals that an amplifier can boost. Sound reaches a thin diaphragm that vibrates, and the way each mic turns that vibration into voltage defines its character. This matters because the same voice or guitar sounds very different through two designs. Understanding that single step, shared by modern microphones and vintage ones, explains why rugged stage microphones, delicate studio microphones and tiny clip-on units all behave differently, and why the audio you capture depends on the sound source as much as the gear.

Types of Microphones: Dynamic, Condenser and Ribbon

Most microphones fall into three families, and finding which microphone type you own narrows down everything else. Each suits a different job, and a well-stocked mic locker usually includes all three. The four short sections below cover them and the specialized microphones that grew out of them.

Dynamic Mics for Loud Sources

A dynamic microphone pairs a diaphragm with a coil and magnet, which makes it rugged and reliable. A dynamic mic handles loud guitar amps and drums, shrugs off handling noise and rejects background noise better than most condensers. It has lower sensitivity, so hold it close to the source. Strong professional mics are available at a modest price, and these mics last for years.

Condenser Mics for Detail

A condenser microphone is more sensitive and captures high-fidelity detail, which is why it dominates the recording studio. A condenser mic comes as a large-diaphragm design, popular for vocals, or a small-diaphragm pencil condenser, preferred for sources with fast transients. The catch is that it also hears everything else. In an untreated room a condenser microphone can pick up reflections, and some budget models sound a little sibilant. Perfect for a quiet podcast recording corner, less so for a noisy kitchen.

Ribbon Mics and Their Velvety Sound

A ribbon microphone uses a thin metal strip and is famous for a smooth, velvety tone. A ribbon mic once needed careful handling because vintage versions were delicate, but modern mics are sturdier and more versatile. Engineers and musician friends often reach for one to soften harsh sources.

Specialized Shotgun, Lavalier and Stereo Mics

A shotgun microphone has a narrow pickup, ideal for dialogue on a set with a boom pole or a camera mount. A lavalier microphone clips to clothing and connects to a wireless microphone receiver, which suits presenters and every vlog or interview. A stereo microphone records two channels at once for ambience. Speakerphone units pack several capsules around a table, and a kick drum mic is tuned for low-end punch. Prices for the content creator range wildly, from cheap clip-ons to professional film rigs, and filmmaker kits often combine all three.

Reading the Polar Pattern of Your Mic

The polar pattern describes the directions your mic hears best. It is one of the quickest ways to place an unknown microphone, because manufacturers print the symbol on the body or the box, and most microphones sold for recording follow the same few symbols. The same pickup pattern drives how much room sound reaches the capsule, so it affects recording quality and how a band, a singer or a speaker will sound at home.

Cardioid: The Common Pickup Pattern

Cardioid is the most common choice. It hears the front, rejects the sides and back, and keeps background noise down. A single cardioid mic is the safe pick for a speaker or singer, and most dynamic mics use it. If you only remember one pattern, remember cardioid.

Omnidirectional for Ambient Sound

An omnidirectional mic hears equally from every side. Omni mics capture the whole room, which suits group recordings and field ambience, but it also invites unwanted sound from anywhere.

Figure-8 for Face-to-Face Interviews

A figure-8 pattern picks up the front and the back while ignoring the sides, so one mic placed between two people records both sides of an interview cleanly.

Multi-pattern and Shotgun Lobar Mics

Some designs let you switch patterns, useful for mixing a studio session where the sound source changes. Shotgun and lobar mics narrow the pickup further, but they bring a slight sensitivity to the proximity effect and off-axis coloration, so check the performance you need before buying.

Mic Specifications That Help You Identify a Microphone

Numbers on a spec sheet look scary, yet a handful of specifications do most of the work when you want to confirm which mic you really own. Each one maps to something you can hear.

SPL Rating and Sound Pressure Level

The SPL figure shows how much sound pressure level the mic can take before it distorts. A singer who hits a loud chorus can overload a sensitive mic, so a high rating means safer capture of loud sources. Pair it with the self-noise, or noise floor, and you get the dynamic range, the gap between the quietest and loudest audio quality the mic can record.

Capsule and Diaphragm Design

The capsule holds the diaphragm, and its size and material shape the sound waves it can follow. Some makers sputter gold onto the diaphragm to keep it thin and even. Bigger capsules tend to sound fuller; smaller ones react faster.

Frequency Response Curve

A frequency response chart shows which pitches get boosted or cut. A bump in the upper mid range adds presence to a voice, while a flat curve stays neutral. Compare the curve printed in the manual with the way your vocals actually sound to confirm the model.

Bass Roll-off and Highpass Filter

A switch marked bass roll-off, sometimes called a highpass filter, trims low rumble. It also tames the proximity effect, the bass boost you get when you speak very close to a directional mic. Its presence is another clue that you hold a studio or broadcast model.

Identifying an Unlabeled Mic: One Desk Drawer Case

I want a clean voice track for a recorded talk, and the only candidate is a black, unmarked mic from the back of a drawer. Which microphone is this, and will it do the job? The answer, as it turns out, is a condenser I can name and place at about 5 cm from my mouth.

First, the clues. The body has a three-pin XLR output and no battery door, so it is wired and needs power from the interface. Under the grille sits a large capsule. Engraved on the underside, almost worn away, I can read "AT2020". I look up the published sheet: cardioid, 144 dB maximum SPL, 20 dB self-noise, and 48 V phantom power (the IEC 61938 standard). Plugging it into an interface with phantom power switched on confirms it: the meter lights up, and with the switch off it stays silent.

Now that I know which mic this is, I check that its specs suit speech. The dynamic range is 144 − 20 = 124 dB, far more than speech needs. What matters is how far my voice sits above the 20 dB noise floor. Speech runs roughly 60 dB SPL at 1 m, and level rises 6 dB each time the distance halves:

  • At 20 cm: 60 + 20 × log10(100 / 20) = 74 dB, which is 54 dB above the noise floor.
  • At 10 cm: 80 dB, which is 60 dB above it.
  • At 5 cm: 86 dB, which is 66 dB above it.

Now that the mic is identified, the number to watch is distance, not the dynamic range. Moving from 20 cm to 5 cm buys 12 dB of extra signal for free, and I recompute by hand with 20 × log10(20 / 5) = 12.04 dB to cross-check the table. I settle on 10 cm as a compromise, because at 5 cm plosives and the proximity effect start to boom.

One edge case when you trust a spec sheet for the mic you have: the 20 dB self-noise figure flatters this mic in my spare room. The room itself sits near 35 dB, so the room, not the capsule, sets the limit, and a lower self-noise model would sound no cleaner. So the identified AT2020 stays, and a test take at 10 cm tells me the rest.

Common Studio Mics You Might Recognize

Most professional studio rooms rely on roughly the same handful of microphones. If yours is one of them, an engineer can tell you how it is normally used. A few appear in nearly every mic locker, and each has its own sweet spot.

Shure SM57 and Shure SM58 Workhorses

The Shure SM57 sets the standard for snare drum and guitar cabinets, and the Shure SM58 is the classic live vocal tool. Their internal parts are similar, and the main difference is the grille, which is why the microphone emoji copies the SM58 shape. Both handle loud transients with ease.

Shure SM7B and Electro-Voice RE20 for Voice

The Shure SM7B and the Electro-Voice RE20 are dynamic favorites for podcasting and broadcast voice. They stay smooth on aggressive vocals, and many recording engineers prefer them over condensers in an imperfect room.

Kick Drum Mics from AKG and Sennheiser

A drummer needs low-end punch, so models such as the AKG D112, Shure Beta 52A and Sennheiser MD421 are tuned for kick drum recording and toms. If your mic has a shaped, boosted response in the lows, it probably belongs to this group.

Neumann, AKG and Royer Studio Classics

Premium condenser microphones and ribbons, from the Neumann U87 to the AKG C414 and Royer 121, are the prestige tier. Their high-fidelity sound comes with a big price, though affordable entry models such as the Blue Yeti give a taste of the same idea for desk use.

Find the Right Microphone for Your Needs

Once you know what you have, you can judge whether it fits the task. Several guides about how to find the right microphone agree on one theme: match the mic to your room, your voice and your use, and never expect fixing in the mix to rescue a wrong choice. Plan to select the perfect microphone for the job first, then fine-tune it. Try a few options if you can, because every voice and every room differs. Run an echo test to confirm your echo cancellation settings are working correctly.

Pick a Vocal Mic for Podcast and Home Studio

For a vocal mic in a home studio, a good dynamic often beats a condenser at the same price. Dynamics hear less of the room, so an untreated space with no acoustic treatment suffers less. Choose the right mic for your voice, and if you chase a bright, airy podcast sound, remember that a darker mic is safer when in doubt. If you choose the right mic and the wrong room, the band or voice will still sound thin, so treat the space too.

Choose a Mic for Conference Room, Classroom and PA System

For a presentation in a meeting room, conference microphones such as a speakerphone or ceiling array keeps audio even around a table. In a classroom or a PA system, a handheld or headset unit keeps the mouth close to the capsule and the sound clear.

Live Performance Venue Mics

If the mic you identified is a handheld dynamic like the SM58, it already suits the stage. A wired handheld is the simple pick, while a wireless setup frees movement during a live performance, so charge the batteries before the doors open.

On a Budget? Use What You Have

A smaller budget does not mean poor results. Many affordable dynamic microphones hold up well, and a camera or phone placed closer to the source can beat an expensive mic placed too far away. Check what mic you already own, keep it near your mouth, and ask the find the right mic questions above before spending more.

Show My Microphone — Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my browser show the wrong microphone?

Browsers default to the system's "Default Input Device" setting, not necessarily the last microphone you plugged in. To check which one the browser is using, open your OS sound settings (Windows: Sound → Input; macOS: System Settings → Sound → Input) and confirm which device is set as the default. After changing it, refresh this page — the device list will update to reflect the new default at the top of the selection order.

What does it mean if my microphone name shows as "Default" or a partial ID?

This usually means the browser hasn't been granted full microphone permission yet, or your browser version limits device label exposure for privacy reasons. Click Allow on the permission prompt to unlock full device names and specifications. In Firefox, you may need to select "Remember this decision" to retain access across sessions. In Brave or Safari, device enumeration may be partially restricted even with permission granted.

Can this tool identify my exact microphone model?

The tool reads exactly what your browser's MediaDevices API reports, which comes from your operating system's audio driver registration. If your OS has the correct driver installed, you'll see the full product name. If the driver is generic or the device uses a shared USB audio class driver, the name may be a chipset or class identifier rather than a brand name. To confirm the exact model, check Device Manager (Windows) or System Information (macOS) for the full hardware descriptor.

My microphone has a "Fair" or "Basic" quality rating — what should I check?

The quality rating is calculated from five factors: sample rate, sample size, channel count, latency, and whether audio processing features are active. A low rating most often means your sample rate is below 44.1 kHz, your sample size is 8-bit, or your mic is operating in a degraded mode (e.g. via a heavily compressed Bluetooth connection). Check your OS audio settings to ensure the device isn't forced into a telephony mode (8 kHz or 16 kHz). For Bluetooth mics, switching the device out of "hands-free" mode into a higher-quality audio profile can significantly improve the rating.
