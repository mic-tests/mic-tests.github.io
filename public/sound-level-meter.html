How to Use This Sound Level Meter Online

Three steps get you a result with this online sound level meter: allow the microphone, watch the gauge, then adjust the settings if you want a different view of the sound. The voice frequency analyzer displays your fundamental pitch, harmonics, and spectral distribution in real time.

Allow Microphone Access

Press start and approve the permission prompt. Your phone, laptop or computer then streams audio to the page, and nothing is uploaded. The same steps work in a mobile browser, so the device in your pocket is enough.

Read the Real-Time Noise Level, Min, Max and Average

The large gauge shows the current reading, while min, max and the average level summarise the session. Hold the device still for about 30 seconds and trust the average level over one brief peak, because real sound keeps shifting.

Choose dBA Frequency Weighting and Peak Hold

Frequency weighting decides which pitches count most. Pick dBA for everyday noise, since the A-weighted curve follows human hearing; dBC (C-weighted) keeps more bass, and dBZ is flat. Peak hold freezes the highest level so you catch a clap. Choose fast or slow response, watch for clipping, and read LAeq as the steady equivalent level.

Use the History and Spectrum Graphs

The history chart plots the last 30 seconds, so you can see when a level rose. The frequency spectrum shows which frequency ranges carry the energy. Set warning thresholds, for example amber at 70 dBA and red at 85 dBA, and note that some tools report LUFS for audio loudness instead.

Checking a Café Grinder Station with a Sound Level Meter Online

Marguerite runs a small café and wants to know whether the espresso grinder corner needs hearing protection for her two baristas, who each stand there about five hours a day. She opens the sound level meter on a phone, sets dBA, clears the history and holds the phone at ear height, 40 cm from the grinder, for a full 60 seconds of steady milling.

The gauge settles around 87 dBA, and the session summary reads an average of 87.4 dBA with a peak near 96 dBA. She trusts the average, not the peak, because a daily limit is about sustained level. Running the 3 dB rule by hand gives T = 8 / 2(87.4-85)/3 ≈ 4.59 hours, or 4 h 36 min. That is shorter than a five-hour shift, so this reading overshoots the NIOSH 85 dBA recommendation by about 24 minutes a day.

My judgement call: since even the lower figure fails, she rotates the baristas off the grinder every two hours and prices a four-decibel enclosure. Sound Meter Accuracy and Calibration

An online sound meter gives an estimate, not a lab result: its accuracy is limited until it is matched to a calibrated source. Treat it as an uncalibrated screening aid rather than a Class 1 instrument from a professional supplier, and never use it for regulatory compliance.

dB SPL vs dBFS: What Your Reading Means

Microphones record digital level (dBFS), where 0 is the maximum. A true dB SPL figure measures air pressure and needs a calibrated microphone and a standard. Until then, compare readings with each other, not with a legal limit. Reported in dBA, the number still reflects your own microphone.

Calibration Offset and a Reference Meter

Play a steady 1kHz sine tone from a tone generator through a speaker at a fixed volume. Hold a reference meter, such as an SPL meter, beside the device and enter the difference as the calibration offset. A calibrator producing 94 dB makes that tone exact. Calibration of headphones follows the same logic.

Common Sources of Error

Phones apply automatic gain control and noise suppression, which flatten loud events, so switch them off where you can. Background noise, the distance to the source and a reflective room all shift a reading, and each microphone and device differs, so a sound meter on one phone can disagree with another. Note your distance, repeat the check three times and keep a short report of the averages.

Online Decibel Meter Readings and Safe Exposure Time

Loudness matters for your hearing, health and safety because harm depends on both level and exposure. Any sound decibel meter reading above 85 dBA starts the clock on safe exposure, and the exposure time shrinks quickly as the level climbs.

The 3 dB Exchange Rate and NIOSH Limits

The 3 dB exchange rate used by NIOSH, part of the CDC, halves the allowed time for every 3 dB above 85 dBA, and the WHO also warns about noise exposure. Keep safe noise levels below 85 dBA over a full day. With level L in dBA, the allowed time in hours is:

T = 8 / (2(L-85)/3)

Worked Example: A Workshop Reading

A woodworker's planer reads 91.5 dBA at ear height, so T = 8 / 2(91.5-85)/3 ≈ 1.78 hours, about 1 h 47 min of unprotected work. Each 3 dB shift halves or doubles that figure:

Hearing Loss, Tinnitus and Hearing Protection

Loud noise bruises the hair cells of the inner ear, causing noise-induced hearing loss that never heals, plus tinnitus. The hearing damage risk grows with level, so wear hearing protection above 85 dBA. Chronic noise pollution also raises stress and disturbs sleep, so treat a sustained meter average above 85 dBA as your cue to act on your health.

Noise Meter Online: Uses at Home and Work

People use an online noise meter to check the environment around them, from a noisy neighbour to a white noise machine.

Bedroom and Sleep Checks

At home, measure the bedroom at night: under about 35 dBA suits sleep. Once the meter confirms a quiet room below 40 dBA, it also suits an online hearing test.

Workplace Noise Checks

In a shared office or workplace, run the meter for the loudest hour and note the average dBA to find the source before it adds stress or hurts concentration.

How Your Browser Measures Sound Pressure Level

The page asks the browser for the microphone, turns loudness in the audio stream into digital samples and shows the result as a number, so it behaves like a software loudness meter built on a sound pressure level estimate.

FFT, RMS and Decibel Mapping

An FFT splits the signal into bands of frequency for weighting, then RMS averages the signal power before it is mapped to a decibel value with the calibration offset.

Privacy: Nothing Leaves Your Browser

Sound Level Meter — Frequently Asked Questions

Is an online sound level meter accurate?

It gives a good estimate for everyday checks, but a phone or laptop microphone is not a calibrated instrument. Readings shift with the device, its gain settings and the distance to the source, so compare readings with each other and calibrate against a reference meter if you need closer numbers.

Why does the meter need microphone access?

The browser can only measure sound if you let the page use your microphone. The audio is analysed on your device and discarded, so nothing is recorded or uploaded.

What is a safe noise level?

Levels under about 70 dB are generally safe all day. At 85 dBA the recommended limit is 8 hours, and above that the allowed time shrinks fast.

What does the 3 dB exchange rate mean?

For every 3 dB above 85 dBA, the safe exposure time is cut in half. The calculator uses T = 8 / 2^((L - 85) / 3), with L in dBA and T in hours.

What is the difference between dBA, dBC and dBZ?

dBA follows how the human ear hears everyday sound, dBC keeps more low-frequency content, and dBZ is flat with no weighting. Exposure limits are written for dBA.

How do I calibrate the reading?

Place a trusted sound level meter beside your microphone in a steady sound, enter both readings, and the calculator works out the offset. You can also type an offset by hand.

What is the difference between dB SPL and dBFS?

dBFS is the digital level your microphone captures, where 0 is the maximum. dB SPL is the real sound pressure in air and needs calibration to a reference.

Can I use this on my phone?

Yes. Any modern mobile browser that allows microphone access works, though phone microphones with automatic gain control can flatten loud sounds.
