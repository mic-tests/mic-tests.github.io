Speak into the voice frequency analyzer and your voice becomes a live frequency spectrum, with your pitch in Hz updating in real time. This free tool is built for speech, so within seconds you can read your fundamental frequency, see your harmonics and judge the tonal balance of your voice.

How to Use the Voice Frequency Analyzer

  1. Click Start and allow microphone access when your browser asks.
  2. Hold a steady vowel such as "ahh" for two to three seconds, then talk in your normal speaking voice.
  3. Read the peak on the graph: the tallest bar is your pitch, and smaller bars to its right are harmonics.
  4. Switch between bars and a line view; the line makes narrow peaks easier to spot, and the spectrogram shows change over time.
  5. Try a whisper as well. Consonants such as "s" and "sh" push energy higher, while a vowel keeps it low, and unvoiced sounds carry no pitch at all.

Keep the microphone a hand's width from your mouth for clean audio, pause during silence, since a noise gate removes background input from the reading, and use a quiet room so ordinary noise does not tilt the result. Use a sound level meter online to check whether your recording environment is quiet enough for clean audio.

Voice Frequency Analysis: Reading Your Results

A voice frequency analysis is a quick voice analysis, not a diagnosis, and it answers one question: which frequencies carry the energy in this sample, and where does the loudest pitch sit? Unvoiced sounds are excluded, so only voiced moments count.

Fundamental Frequency and Your Speaking Pitch

The fundamental frequency (F0) is how many times per second your vocal folds open and close, measured in hertz. Say you hold "ahh" and the display settles at 137 Hz: your vocal cords vibrate 137 times each second. The nearest musical note is C#3 at 138.59 Hz, so your voice pitch is about 20 cents flat. A doubling of frequency is one octave, so 274 Hz would sit exactly an octave up.

cents = 1200 × log₂(f / (fnote))

Harmonics, Overtones and Tonal Character

Harmonics are whole-number multiples of F0: a 137 Hz tone has harmonics at 274, 411 and 548 Hz. These overtones shape the tonal character you hear. The vocal tract boosts some of them, forming formants, and that resonance is why a warm voice and a bright voice differ even at equal pitch. On the graph they appear as evenly spaced peaks that shrink toward the right, like the waveform's finer detail.

f_n = n × F_0

Median, Mean and Typical Range of a Sample

Read the median first: it is the middle value of the sample and ignores stray spikes. The mean averages every reading, so a few high values drag it up. The typical range runs from the 10th to the 90th percentile; if yours spans 137 to 184 Hz, the spread is 12 × log2(184 ÷ 137) ≈ 5.1 semitones (one semitone is a twelfth of an octave). The median also stays put when you cough. Good pitch accuracy shows as a narrow band, while a slow upward or downward slide signals pitch drift. Compare only samples taken the same way.

Checking a Podcast Microphone with the Voice Frequency Analyzer

I'm recording a podcast intro and want to know whether my voice pitch stays consistent between a seated take and a standing take, because the editor says the second one sounds "brighter". I open the analyzer, read the passage aloud for 15 seconds each time, and note the median rather than the mean, since one cough would skew an average.

Take one, seated, gives a median of 118.4 Hz. That sits comfortably inside the 85–180 Hz range usually quoted for adult men, and the nearest note is A#2 at 116.54 Hz, so I'm about 27 cents sharp of it. Take two, standing, returns 131.9 Hz. The voice frequency analysis tells me the gap is 13.5 Hz, which is 12 × log2(131.9 ÷ 118.4) ≈ 1.87 semitones, a rise of about 11%.

TakeMedianChange
Seated118.4 Hzbaseline
Standing131.9 Hz+1.87 semitones
Standing, mic 15 cm further away126.7 Hz+1.17 semitones

For the sensitivity rerun I change one input: the microphone distance. Moving the capsule 15 cm further away drops the median to 126.7 Hz, so about 0.7 semitone of the "brightness" was my posture and the rest was how the microphone and my voice interact, not a new voice. What stands out is that the number to watch is the median across takes, not one peak.

The trap is the octave error: 2 × 118.4 = 236.8 Hz. If the display jumps to 236.8 Hz, the detector has locked onto the second harmonic, and that reading would wrongly land in the typical female range. A jump of exactly twelve semitones is the giveaway, so I repeat the take instead of trusting it, because a spike like that should never be averaged in. My decision: record the final take standing, with the microphone 15 cm further back, and rerun the analyzer once more to confirm the median stays near 127 Hz.

Frequency Spectrum Bands: Bass, Mid, Presence, Brilliance

The analyzer splits the sound into four frequency bands, using an FFT, so you can see where your audio signal is strong or thin. Each band is measured in dB and in kHz terms at its upper edge.

BandRangeWhat it adds
Bass80–300 HzBody and depth; too much sounds boomy
Mid300 Hz–2 kHzIntelligibility: vowels and warmth
Presence2–4 kHzForward clarity and loudness cut
Brilliance4 kHz and upAir and sibilance, the crisp edge of "s"

When you open the analyzer before speaking, a spike at 50 or 60 Hz lands in the bass band and is mains hum, not your voice, so check for it when the low end looks strong; a spike 10 dB above its neighbours is typical. A flat brilliance band tells you the sound is dull.

Dominant Frequency and the Spectrogram View

The dominant frequency is the single strongest bin in the spectrum, and the spectrogram plots it over time so you can watch it move as you speak.

Voice Pitch Ranges by Voice Type

Your voice type depends on vocal fold length and mass, so there is no correct number; the ranges below are common patterns for singing and speech.

Speaking Frequency for Adult Men and Women

Adult men usually speak between 85 and 180 Hz, a deep bass near 85–100 Hz and a tenor near 150–180 Hz, with a baritone between them. Adult women usually speak between 165 and 255 Hz, from alto near 165–190 Hz to soprano near 220–260 Hz. The male and female ranges overlap around 150–200 Hz, so pitch alone cannot name a speaker as male or female.

How Voice Frequency Changes with Age

Before puberty most voices sit near 250–300 Hz. The larynx then grows and male pitch drops sharply, female pitch only a little. A child's reading near 250–300 Hz is normal on the analyzer, and your vocal range for singing is a separate measure.

Practical Uses for a Sound Spectrum Analyzer

The analyzer's spectrum view works as an audio spectrum analyzer for room acoustics and any music or recording where you cannot hear what is wrong with your voice. Play a sine wave or pink noise and watch how your room changes it.

Fixing Thin Sound on Video Calls

If you sound tinny on video calls or a conference call, speak into the analyzer and compare the bands. A weak mid band means your microphone or its position is cutting your voice. A missing presence band is common with laptop microphones. A dominant bass band points to proximity effect, and a dip near 1–3 kHz suggests comb filtering from a reflective desk.

Vocal Training and Pitch Monitoring

Singers and vocal coaches hold a note and watch the Hz readout: it should stay steady. The tuner view shows the closest musical note against A4 = 440 Hz, a standard tuning reference, so note detection tells you how many cents you are off. A held note and plain speech are different samples, so label them separately.

Limits of a Pitch Detector and Voice Checker

A poor microphone adds noise that can distort the analyzer's reading. A pitch detector can mistake a strong harmonic for F0 and report a result an octave too high. If a reading suddenly doubles, repeat the sound. Breathy or very quiet speech may give no reading, and the voice band for telephone speech is far narrower than this view. The free background noise analyzer identifies specific noise sources — fan hum, mains hum, and reflections — by frequency.

  • Run each voice frequency test, voice hz test or voice pitch test with one clear sound and the same microphone, and compare the median across takes.
  • Do not treat one result as who you are; a voice checker measures a sample, not identity or health.
  • Save readings to CSV and repeat before judging any change.

Voice Frequency Analyzer — Frequently Asked Questions

What is a normal voice frequency for speaking?

Adult men typically speak at 85–180 Hz (average: ~120 Hz); adult women at 165–255 Hz (average: ~210 Hz). There is no "correct" speaking frequency — it is determined by the length and mass of your vocal folds, which are set by genetics. However, habitual pitch can be consciously raised or lowered with training, and many people unconsciously shift their speaking pitch depending on context (formal vs casual, authoritative vs empathetic).

Why does my fundamental frequency change throughout the day?

Voice frequency is influenced by hydration, muscle warm-up, and hormonal cycles. Immediately after waking, vocal folds are less hydrated and the voice typically runs 5–15 Hz lower than usual. After speaking for 10–15 minutes, folds warm up and frequency stabilises. Dehydration throughout the day causes progressive stiffening that raises the fundamental frequency slightly. Menstrual cycle hormones in women can cause measurable frequency shifts. Stress increases laryngeal muscle tension, which raises pitch.

Can I use the voice frequency analyzer to tune my singing voice?

Yes — sustain a note, read the fundamental frequency in Hz, and compare it to the target note frequency (A4 = 440 Hz, C4 = 261.6 Hz, etc.). The "Closest Note" display and cents deviation reading show you how close to the target pitch you are. However, for dedicated pitch training, a chromatic tuner designed for vocal use gives more musical feedback. This voice frequency analyzer is more useful for understanding the full spectral character of your voice than for precise pitch-by-pitch tuning exercises.

Why is my fundamental frequency display showing "--" or fluctuating wildly?

The analyzer needs a sustained, tonal sound to detect a clear fundamental frequency. Background noise, consonants, whispers, and ambient room sounds produce complex, atonal spectra where pitch detection algorithms struggle to identify a single dominant frequency. Sustain a clear "ahhh" vowel and hold it steady for 2–3 seconds to get a stable reading. Also check that the room is reasonably quiet — constant noise above 60 dB can swamp the microphone signal and make pitch detection unreliable.

What do the Hz numbers mean for my voice quality?

The Hz value on its own does not indicate voice quality — it only indicates pitch. A deep voice at 100 Hz is not better or worse than a higher voice at 220 Hz. What matters for voice quality is the distribution of frequency energy: a well-balanced voice with strong presence (2–4 kHz) is clear and intelligible; a voice with weak mids is muffled; one with excessive bass is boomy. Use the frequency distribution bands below the spectrum graph to assess quality, not just the fundamental frequency reading.
