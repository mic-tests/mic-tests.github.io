Headphone Test Online: How to Run It in Your Browser

Because it works as a headphone test in browser, nothing installs and no sound leaves your device. Treat it as a quick diagnostic for headphones: it catches faults long before a warranty runs out and protects your hearing from harsh, mislabeled headphones. Uncertain about what speakers do i have? The tool reads your system's audio output devices directly from the browser.

  1. Plug in or pair your headphones, preferably over a wired connection.
  2. Start quietly: set the system and tool volume low, then raise it slowly.
  3. Play each test tone and move the pan control, the panorama slider, from left to right.
  4. Note anything odd and read the matching section below.

Any reliable headphone tool can reveal wear that you cannot hear in everyday listening, because familiar songs hide small sound faults.

Left-Right Test: Are Your Earbuds Wired Correctly?

Begin with the left right audio test, also called a left right sound test: play the left channel, then the right channel, and each should sound only in its own ear. A left right test headphones pass means your headphones are wired correctly. If it fails, check these results:

  • The channels are swapped or the earcups sit the wrong way round: sound arrives in the opposite ear.
  • Both sides play together: the signal is mono, often from a 3-pole plug in a 4-pole socket or a cheap adapter.
  • One side is quieter: open your sound settings and center the balance slider before blaming the headphones; check the wiring next.

Run an earphone test or a headphones test the same way for any model, since the channels logic never changes.

Stereo Test and Channels Check

The stereo test plays both channels of your headphones together, so a centered tone confirms matched output and good stereo balance.

Frequency Test: Your Headphones From Bass to Treble

The frequency test uses a sine sweep or a frequency generator to cover the hearing range, roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz, also written 20,000Hz. In hertz, bass extension shows how low the drivers reach and treble extension how high; a good frequency range in headphones covers both without gaps, as frequency is the core of every musical notes scale.

BandRangeWhat to listen for
Sub-bass20-60 HzA felt rumble, not a rattle
Bass60-250 HzWarm, tight thump
Mid (speech range)250 Hz-4 kHzClear, natural voices
Treble4-20 kHzCrisp detail without hiss

If the highest tones vanish, suspect your hearing loss from age first, and an odd skipping sweep may point to aliasing in your sound card.

Bass Test: Sweep the Full Range

A slow sweep in a bass test shows dips or peaks; the shape of the frequency response matters more than any single note.

Spectral Flatness and Earbud Insert Test for Earphones

Spectral flatness means no frequency sounds louder than another. The earbud insert test matters most for any in-ear design, because insertion depth reshapes the frequency response; a headphones (and earphones) test at a very low level reveals the depth that sounds flattest.

Driver Quality Test: Driver Matching on Your Headphones

Driver quality depends on tolerances. Driver matching means both drivers respond equally, so a sweep stays centered; a wandering tone points to mismatched units. The bass shaker sweep exposes any rattle or rattling part, and buzz or buzzing is the other common sign. A square wave and short bursts uncover resonances, distortion and harmonic distortion; reduce the level when the test tone turns rough.

Polarity Test

A low tone should push both drivers outward together; weak bass suggests reversed polarity.

Phase Test

An in-phase tone sounds centered, while out-of-phase sound feels thin and diffuse.

A Left Right Audio Test on a Pair of Used Earbuds

The decision: keep a second-hand pair of earbuds bought for $38.40, or return them within the seller's 14-day window. I plug them into my laptop with the cable, set the tool volume to 35% and open the left right audio test first.

Left plays a clear tone. Right plays it too, but a reading of the tone against my ear says it is quieter. That gap is the whole story, so I put a number on it by ear-matching the right side against a reference tone until they sound level: the right earbud needs about 3.5 dB less attenuation. Using amplitude ratio = 10(-3.5/20), the quieter side is playing at 0.668 of the louder side's amplitude. Roughly 1 dB is the point where most listeners begin to notice a level difference, so 3.5 dB is clearly audible, not imagined.

Then the sweep: the tone stays steady until it fades out at 15.8 kHz. Against the 20 kHz ceiling most spec sheets quote, that is log2(20/15.8) = 0.34 octave missing, a minor treble shortfall that many adults share with perfectly healthy earbuds, so I do not count it against them.

CheckResultVerdict
Left vs right level3.5 dB gap (0.668 ratio)Fault or source issue
Treble limit15.8 kHz (0.34 octave short)Acceptable
Swap test (earbuds reversed)Quiet side stays on the same earbudEarbud fault

The sensitivity rerun decides it: I swap the earbuds between ears and repeat. The quiet sound moves with the earbud, so the laptop's balance is innocent and the right driver is the problem. That is my judgement call: the number to watch is the gap, not the treble limit. One caveat: a gap that stays on one side instead points to the source, so a swap test always comes before any return. Next action: message the seller today with the 3.5 dB result and request the refund before day 14.

Soundstage Test and Stereo Imaging on a Gaming Headset

Gaming depends on the soundstage your gaming headset creates, and headphones differ widely: good stereo imaging places a tone outside your head, and soundstage imaging on a grid proves it. The binaural test adds depth, which helps with 3D audio and spatial audio. Over-ear headphones usually win for gaming and studio use; on-ear headphones trade space for portability, and soft ear cups and fresh ear pads help the seal.

Dynamic Range and Isolation in Closed Headphones

The dynamic range step of this headphone tool measures isolation: play noise, then a quiet voiceover that steps down in dBFS. The lowest level you still catch shows how well your headphones shut out the room. If the quiet steps vanish early, either your closed or open design leaks sound or the room is loud, and loud decibels from speakers nearby will skew the result.

Audio Latency and Microphone Test for Wireless Headphones

Audio latency is the delay between action and sound. Wireless links over Bluetooth add latency, and dropouts mid-test mean interference. For podcast or recording work, run the microphone check as well.

Headphones Not Working? Troubleshooting Fixes

When headphones not working is the problem, start troubleshooting with a firmly seated cable in the jack and a clean plug; earwax on tips quiets one side. Static or crackling when you move the cable means the wire is failing, and your headset may need repair. Use the speaker test to rule out the output device. Before any recording session, a sound level meter online confirms your ambient noise floor is within acceptable limits.

Judge Your Headphones' Sound Quality With Music

After the tones, play familiar music. Good sound quality shows in clear vocals, clarity without mud, a bass thump that stays tight and smoothness across the sweep. Listening to a concert recording through a proper amplifier shows how the audio quality holds up, and good polarity keeps the stage intact. Test your headphones every few months.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a headphone test check?

It plays test tones so you can check the left and right channels, balance, frequency response, sweeps, short bursts, a square wave and phase, all in your browser.

Why do I hear sound on only one side?

Play the left, right and stereo tones and center the pan control. If one side stays silent or quiet, check the plug, the ear cup fit and your system balance slider before suspecting the driver.

How do I set a safe volume?

Set the system volume low, keep the tool at a comfortable level, and raise it slowly. The tool starts at 20%, and wired headphones give the most reliable results.

What frequency range should good headphones reproduce?

Human hearing spans roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Good headphones play that range smoothly; the upper limit falls with age, so a lower treble limit on every pair can point to your hearing.

What is the pan control for?

Pan shifts the signal between left and right so you can check the balance of your headphones. This calculator shows the resulting channel levels using an equal-power pan.

What do the burst and square wave tests detect?

Short bursts reveal distortion or overshoot, and a low square wave exposes rattling parts and enclosure resonances. Reduce the volume if something rattles.

Can I use Bluetooth headphones?

Yes, but Bluetooth can add latency and trim the highest frequencies. Use a cable for precise results.

Do I need to install anything?

No. The test runs in your browser and does not upload any audio.
