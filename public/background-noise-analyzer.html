The free online noise analyzer listens through your microphone in your browser, needs no registration, and shows your overall noise level in dBFS plus how the sound splits across frequency bands, so you get a clear baseline measurement of the audio in your environment.

How to Use the Background Noise Analyzer

The online background noise test runs on the Web Audio API and getUserMedia, so your microphone audio is analyzed on your device only. The free voice frequency analyzer is useful for vocal training, call quality checks, and microphone assessment.

  1. Pick your microphone and set the sample duration.
  2. Keep speech out of the sample and stay quiet in your usual environment.
  3. Read the result, change one thing and compare it against your baseline.

Start the Test and Allow Microphone Access

Press start, then allow microphone access when your browser asks. If nothing happens, close Zoom or any other app holding the mic and retry. The history log keeps each run, and a threshold alert flags a noisy space.

Stay Quiet During the Sample

A short run suits steady noise such as a fan; a longer one catches intermittent noise, though it cannot promise a passing truck shows up. Privacy note: nothing is uploaded.

Read Your Ambient Noise Level

The average summarizes your ambient noise over the run, while peak, minimum and maximum show how much the levels moved. Your background noise level is only trustworthy next to another run from the same mic, because an uncalibrated meter shows relative digital full-scale values, not sound pressure level.

Leq, L10 and L90 Explained

Leq is the energy average of your noise measurement. L10 is the level exceeded 10% of the time (the peaks) and L90 the level exceeded 90% of the time, which approximates the quiet floor. A big gap between them means conditions keep changing.

Frequency Spectrum and Room Noise

The frequency spectrum of your audio shows what the room noise is made of. The spectrum analyzer view, with a frequency analyzer readout of the dominant frequency in hertz and a spectrogram over time, helps you trace a problem back to its source instead of guessing.

Frequency Bands: Low, Mid and High

Bass and sub bass sit in the low band, speech in the mid band, and treble in the high band. If one share jumps after you fix something, check the overall level too, since percentages shift when another component drops.

Electrical Hum and Low-Frequency Rumble

A spike near 50 or 60 Hz suggests mains hum from wiring or a transformer, and its harmonics repeat above it. Rumble from HVAC, air conditioning or traffic piles up in the same low band, while hiss lives in the high band.

Octave Bands and A-Weighting

Octave bands group the spectrum in proportional steps; white noise and pink noise are the usual test signals. A-weighting trims bass and treble to mimic your ear, so a weighting choice changes the number you read.

Noise Floor vs Microphone Noise

Your noise floor is the quietest level your setup can show. It includes microphone noise (self-noise), input gain and mic gain choices, and any clipping, so the lowest reading is not always the space's true acoustic level. Keep gain fixed when you compare. Make a ten-second recording with your usual microphone, then repeat with the gain halved; if the audio floor barely moves, the microphone noise, not your space, sets the limit. A noise floor analyzer works the same way.

Common Types of Background Noise

Fans and appliances give a steady noise source that shows as a flat band of energy, mains hum is a narrow spike near 50 or 60 Hz, and hiss lifts the high band. Clicks and interference appear as sudden peaks, and artifacts add acoustic problems that the room tone itself does not explain.

Signal-to-Noise Ratio Guidelines

SNR is your talker level minus the floor. For example, a talker at -18 dB over a floor of -61 dB gives 43 dB:

Calibrate Your Background Noise Test

Calibration ties dBFS to a real sound pressure level. Match a trusted reference meter during steady sound at the same position and weighting, then save the offset for that microphone. Say the reference meter reads 38.0 dBA while the page shows -57.0 on its digital scale: the calibration offset is +95.0 dB for that microphone, weighting and gain. A later reading of -49.0 then corresponds to roughly 46 dBA. Repeat your background noise level test whenever the audio interface, gain or position changes, because that calibration no longer applies; without it, compare runs only against each other.

The 10 dB Rule Explained

The 10 dB rule says ambient sound should sit at least 10 dB below what you measure. A source at 60 dB needs a room at 50 dB or lower for accuracy within about 0.5 dB.

Spare-Room Noise Measurement: Window Open vs Closed

Dana opens the analyzer to check whether her spare room, a planned voice-over booth, stays under 35 dBA, the ANSI/ASA S12.60 classroom limit and a fair yardstick for speech work.

She first calibrates against a handheld meter that reads 36.9 dBA while the page shows -54.4 dBFS, giving an offset of 36.9 + 54.4 = +91.3 dB for this microphone, gain and A-weighting. Then she runs the analyzer for two 60-second samples, changing only the window.

The closed-window result of 33.4 dBA clears the 35 dBA target by 1.6 dB, while the open window misses it by 6.1 dB. Closing the window removes 7.7 dB of street noise, and that is the number to watch: it moves the signal-to-noise ratio from 28.7 dB to 36.4 dB. She cross-checks by hand, adding the offset to each reading, and both match the meter's trend.

The edge case is the refrigerator in the next room. Its compressor cycles, so a 60-second Leq can land in a quiet stretch and flatter the room. Dana's L10 sits well above her L90, which tells her the floor is not steady, so she repeats the run for five minutes before trusting 33.4. Her offset also only holds at this gain: touching the input level by even 6 dB shifts every calibrated figure, so she tapes the gain knob in place. Her next action is a fresh five-minute closed-window run, then a draught strip for the window frame.

Noise Reduction Fixes for Ambient Sound

Start with the source of the audio problem, rerun the analyzer after every change to compare against your baseline, then use noise reduction software only for what remains. Heavy processing adds artifacts, so a gentle noise gate beats aggressive cleanup. The online mic recorder runs locally in your browser — your audio is never sent to any server.

  • If the low band dominates, switch off the heater, move away from appliances and rerun.
  • Put a headset or directional mic close to your voice for better audio.
  • Add acoustic treatment to cut reverb in a recording room.

Noise Suppression and Auto Gain Control

Noise suppression in meeting apps hides steady background noise, but it answers a different question from measuring the room. Switch off auto gain control and echo cancellation for the test, since they move the numbers.

Fix Fans, Hiss and Echo

A fan shows as a steady low-band level and hiss as high-band energy. Lower the input gain to cut noise at the source, then confirm in the analyzer that the level dropped; soft panels soften reflections.

Room Tone Checks Before Calls and Recordings

Run a check before video calls, meetings or a podcast, and again after you change speaker position or move to a quiet room. Audio quality depends on your environment as much as your microphone, so test the same environment you will use for recording, at the hour you will use it.

Is Your Audio Recorded?

No. Measurement happens in your browser, so no audio recording is saved or sent, and you can revoke microphone permission at any time.

Background Noise Analyzer — Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good background noise level for recording?

Many home recording setups aim for a calibrated room level around 35 dBA or lower and a signal-to-noise ratio of at least 40 dB, so your voice sits well above the floor.

Is this an absolute decibel meter?

Not by itself. A browser reads relative digital levels in dBFS. Matching a trusted reference meter gives an offset that converts the reading to an estimated dBA for that microphone, gain and weighting.

What is the 10 dB rule for background noise?

For a measurement to be accurate within about 0.5 dB, the background must sit at least 10 dB below the source you measure. This calculator checks that gap from your two levels.

Why does my noise reading change between runs?

Fans, air conditioning, keyboard clicks, passing traffic and automatic gain control all move the level. Take several samples with one change at a time and compare them.

Which sample duration should I choose?

Ten seconds suits steady noise; 30 to 60 seconds gives intermittent sounds a chance to appear. A longer run still cannot guarantee a rare event is captured.

Does the weighting change the result?

The weighting is a property of the reading you enter. A-weighting follows human hearing, C-weighting keeps more bass, and Z is flat. Use the same one for the reference meter and your offset.

Is my audio recorded or uploaded?

This calculator only uses the numbers you type. A measurement tool running in your browser analyzes microphone audio locally and does not need to store it.
