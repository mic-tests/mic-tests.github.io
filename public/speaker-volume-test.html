What a Speaker Volume Test Checks

A speaker volume test plays signals at known steps so you can judge how loud, clean and even your audio equipment sounds. It confirms that your loudspeakers and amplifier respond when you raise or lower the level, and it lets you compare quiet and louder passages in stereo. A sagging channel or a fuzzy signal shows up fast. Use a left right audio test whenever one side seems weak. Use the stereo test to verify your left and right channels are correctly separated.

How This Free Sound Test Online Works

This sound test online uses the Web Audio API to generate every signal inside your browser, so nothing is uploaded and your privacy stays intact. A sine tone, a frequency sweep and other test tones sound from your device on demand. White noise and pink noise act as reference signals: white noise adds a steady hiss across the spectrum, while pink noise is gentler to listen to. Playback starts only when you press a button, and each tone stops when you stop it. Pick a tone, raise the noise gradually and listen.

Speaker Test Options: Channels, Bass and Sweeps

Each check below targets one channel or one frequency band. Run the channels first, then move down to bass and across the range.

Left Right Sound Test for Channel Balance

Click Left, then Right, and confirm each sound comes from the matching side. The left and right buttons expose swapped channels, a silent right channel or a weak left channel. Equal loudness means good stereo balance, while an imbalanced result pulls the image to one side. If both stereo channels play but sound uneven, adjust the balance controls on your system and confirm the channel mapping matches your layout.

Bass Test and Subwoofer Response

Play the 60 Hz tone, then sweep the low frequency range from 20 Hz to 250 Hz. A subwoofer should keep going into sub-bass, while laptop speakers fade out early. If you hear no bass response below 80 Hz, your system has limited reach. Any resonance or rattling means something near the speaker is vibrating, so move it and repeat.

Frequency Sweep for Frequency Response

The log sweep glides from 20 Hz to 20 kHz and shows the frequency response of your speakers. Listen for a smooth rise through the midrange and treble; dips, spikes or distortion mark weak spots. Judge dynamic range by how clearly the quietest moment stays audible against the loudest. The spectrum spans about ten octaves, so a gap at any point is easy to hear.

Headphone Test and Earbuds

Wear your headphones and run the same sweep and channel checks. A good headset covers the full hearing range, while cheap earbuds lose deep bass. Tones should stay clear in both ears. Your hearing also changes with age, so treat this as a screening, not a hearing loss diagnosis; a clinical audiogram is the proper route.

Stereo Polarity and Audio Test Files

The audio test files here check polarity, which reflects how your speakers and amplifier are wired. When one speaker moves in while the other moves out, speaker polarity is wrong and bass cancels at the listening position. Reversed connecting wires cause it, so swap the wiring on one speaker only. Good polarity sharpens stereo imaging and sound localization. Room acoustics still shape what you hear from each seat.

In Phase and Out of Phase Check

Play the in phase and out of phase pairs from a central listening position. The correct pair sounds fuller, with more stereo separation, like a music recording playing in front of you; the other feels twisted inside your head and loses bass. If the out of phase pair sounds better, reverse one speaker's wires.

How to Interpret Your Audio Check Results

Read each result against what you hear. Both channels should sound clear and equal, and a sweep that drops off sharply at either end signals weak frequency response. A tone that wobbles or crackles can mean distortion. Rerun the same audio check after any change so the comparison stays fair, and note the frequency response you hear at each step.

Microphone Level Meter

The microphone meter shows live input with a peak hold. Speak normally and watch the level meter respond; no movement means a muted or disconnected microphone, and constant high readings suggest feedback. Keep peaks under clipping, around 70-90%. Use it to confirm each volume step actually reaches the microphone before a call.

Accuracy and Limitations of an Online Sound Test in Your Browser

Your speakers, headphones, system processing and ears all shape what you hear, and browser audio is not calibrated, so meters and levels here are relative, never exact readings. Use a calibrated sound meter for any acoustic or production decision. Treat this page as a first check of your audio system, equipment and hardware, and use proper calibration tools when precision matters.

From Left Right Audio Test to a 65 dB Night Level

I need my desk speakers quiet enough for 11 pm work without losing clarity, so the decision is a slider position. I start with the left and right check: both sides come up evenly, so the setup is sound. Then I play a steady tone at 100% and read my phone's meter from the chair: 82.6 dB.

That is 2.4 dB under the 85 dB occupational exposure guideline, so full volume is already the ceiling, not a safe default. My target for late-night work is 65 dB. The gap is 65 − 82.6 = −17.6 dB, and inverting the level formula gives an amplitude ratio of 10−17.6/20 = 0.132. So the slider belongs near 13%, which matches what the volume test showed me: low steps are far quieter than their percentages suggest.

I rerun the same calculation with nearby positions to see how touchy it is:

The number to watch is the move from 13% to 20%: seven points of slider add only 3.7 dB, so fine steps near the bottom matter most. I set 13%, replay the tone and read 65.2 dB, a 0.2 dB miss that I accept.

One caveat: the formula assumes the slider scales amplitude linearly, and many operating systems use a curved taper, so the percentage can land elsewhere. Phone microphones also read bass poorly, so I measure with a mid-range tone, not the 60 Hz one, and treat the result as a rough guide rather than precise data. If the reading disagrees with the formula by more than 3 dB, I trust the meter and adjust the slider until the target is met.

When to Run a Sound Test

Run a quick audio test before team meetings, after changing audio settings or when something just sounds off. It takes under a minute and shows whether the problem is your speakers, your device or the call.

Before Video Calls and Gaming

Confirm your speakers and headset are clear before important calls or a gaming session, because positional audio depends on clean stereo separation. Catching a dead side now beats hearing teammates say they cannot hear you.

Testing New Headphones

When you unbox new headphones or speakers, check the channels, the sweep and any subwoofer before your return window closes. A defect found early costs nothing; one found late costs the purchase.

Troubleshooting Results From an Audio Tester

If an audio tester shows one-sided audio, start with the simplest causes: cables, balance controls and your audio settings. Move the plug to another port, try another cable and rerun the test. Troubleshooting in that order separates a faulty wire from a software setting. For subwoofer calibration, the bass test gives you precise low-frequency control.

Bluetooth Headset and Audio Driver Fixes

If one side stays silent or the sweep distorts over Bluetooth, confirm the headset is the selected output, since a paired device can still route sound elsewhere. Then update the audio driver and allow the browser's microphone permission. Outdated software is a common cause of distortion and missing channels.

Safe Volume Levels During Speaker Testing

Warning: loud sounds can cause hearing damage after even one brief exposure. Start each speaker testing session at a reasonable level and raise it in small steps.

Sound Tester Glossary: Key Audio Terms

A sound tester relies on a few terms worth knowing.

Frequency response
The span of pitches a speaker reproduces, from deep bass up to 20 kHz.
Stereo balance
The relative loudness of the left and right channels; equal means centred sound.
Bass frequency
Low frequency pitches below about 250 Hz that need a subwoofer or large cone.
White noise
Equal power at every frequency, heard as a hiss.
Pink noise
Equal energy per octave, closer to how music is balanced.

Frequently Asked Questions About the Speaker Test Online

Is the online speaker test safe for my speakers?

Yes, as long as you start at a moderate volume and avoid sustained maximum-volume playback. The test tones are standard sine waves and noise signals — the same types used in professional audio calibration. Start at 25–50% volume and only increase if needed. If you hear distortion at any level, stop immediately and reduce volume.

Why does the speaker test sound different at different volume levels?

All speakers have a non-linear frequency response that changes with volume. At low volumes, bass frequencies sound thinner (the equal-loudness contour effect — the ear is less sensitive to bass at low levels). At high volumes, the speaker's physical limits cause harmonic distortion. This is normal speaker behaviour, not a fault.

Can I use this as a subwoofer test?

For subwoofer testing, the bass test is better suited — it covers 20–200 Hz with a dedicated sweep. The 1 kHz tone and frequency sweep in this speaker test focus on the mid-range. That said, playing white noise through all speakers simultaneously (including the subwoofer) gives a useful overall balance check.

Why does the speaker test only play through one channel?

Check your OS balance control first — it may be set fully left or right. Also verify your cable: a mono-to-stereo adaptor or a partially-inserted 3.5 mm jack will only output to one channel. If using Bluetooth, check that the speaker is connected in stereo mode, not mono/hands-free profile.

Does the speaker test work with Bluetooth speakers?

Yes. Select your Bluetooth speaker as the audio output device in your OS sound settings before running the test. Note that Bluetooth audio introduces 50–200 ms of latency — the tone will play with a slight delay after you click Play. This is normal and does not affect the volume or quality test results.

What is white noise and why is it used in speaker testing?

White noise is a random signal that contains all audible frequencies simultaneously at equal power. It's used in speaker testing because it exercises the full frequency range of the driver in a single pass. If a speaker has a weak tweeter, the white noise will sound muffled. If the woofer is damaged, the noise will have a hollow, thin quality.
