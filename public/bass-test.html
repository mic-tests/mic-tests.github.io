Sub-Bass vs Mid-Bass in a Low Frequency Range Test

A low frequency range test spans about 10Hz to 200Hz, in two halves. Sub-bass (20 Hz to 60 Hz) is the rumble you feel in movie theaters and clubs; the tactile vibration matters more than what you hear. Mid-bass (60 Hz to 200 Hz) carries the punch and warmth of kick drums and a bass guitar. Anywhere in the low frequency range, the human hearing range sets the floor at 20 Hz.

Bass Response of Subwoofers, Speakers and Headphones

Reproducing the lowest frequency means moving air. A subwoofer with large drivers in a sealed enclosure holds 30Hz cleanly, laptop speakers roll off near 80Hz, and budget earbuds stop around 40Hz. Premium headphones and over-ear headphones reach 20 Hz because the sealed ear canal acts as a pressure chamber that lifts the low-end by 12 dB per octave. Repeat the bass test on each audio device and compare its frequency response by noting where the tone disappears.

Wavelength Formula for Your Lowest Frequency

Say the sweep first becomes audible at 34 Hz on your speakers: λ = c / f = 343 / 34 ≈ 10.1 m A wave that long needs big drivers, which is why small ones fade first.

Using the Bass Tester on Two Pairs of Headphones

Priya is choosing which headphones to take on a long flight: her over-ear pair or her earbuds. She wants whichever plays deeper, so she runs the sweep on each, in a quiet room, at the same volume, and writes down the number the voiceover reads when the rising tone first becomes audible.

The over-ear pair gives 27.4 Hz; the earbuds give 41.0 Hz. Dividing the speed of sound by each frequency gives the wavelengths: 343 ÷ 27.4 = 12.5 m and 343 ÷ 41.0 = 8.4 m. Against the common benchmark that headphones hearing 30 Hz or lower without distortion have excellent bass, the over-ear pair clears the bar and the earbuds do not.

The first audible tone jumps to 52.0 Hz, nearly an octave higher, because a leak breaks the sealed ear canal chamber that gives headphones their low-end lift. That is the edge case worth knowing: a single result is only valid for the fit you tested, so a loose cushion or glasses frame can make good headphones look weak.

My read is that the seal matters more than the model here. The number to watch is the sealed result, and she decides to keep the over-ear pair, re-seat the cushions over her glasses before the flight, and rerun the sweep once more; if it still reads above 30 Hz, she will pack the earbuds as backup.

Spot Harmonic Distortion With a Sine Wave

During the test, tones below 20 Hz should stay inaudible. If you hear something at 15 Hz, your speaker or subwoofer is adding distortion rather than playing the signal. Rattling means loose parts, and a sudden volume drop shows roll off. A sine wave keeps the waveform clean, so any harmonic distortion you hear is the gear's own, and the transient response stays easy to judge when you are tuning. Judge performance and quality by how undistorted the output stays.

Room Acoustics and Low-Frequency Tones

If the tone drops out or swells at certain spots, your room, not your gear, is often the cause: standing waves create dead spots and boom. To see what speakers do i have, the browser's MediaDevices API identifies every output endpoint in seconds.

Speaker Placement and the Subwoofer Crawl

Put a woofer in a corner so the walls reinforce it, or try the subwoofer crawl: place it at your seat and crawl around to find the smoothest spot. Rerun the test at each position and keep the one where the tone stays audible and even.

Equalizer, Bass Boost and Hardware Upgrades

If the tone drops out early, use an equalizer to lift 40Hz to 100Hz gently, then retest after every change; a bass boost option helps, but over-boosting adds distortion. Still thin? Add a dedicated subwoofer to carry the lowest notes, since your audio system then gains headroom.

Safe Volume for Any Frequency Test

High SPL at extreme lows can damage audio equipment, so keep the sound modest. Streaming bass test songs for a car or gaming rig is fine for music and movies, but a bass test for speakers belongs in a pure free online frequency test like this one, where hearing and gear both stay safe.

Frequently Asked Questions About the Bass Test

What is a bass test?

A bass test plays low-frequency tones through your speakers or headphones so you can hear the lowest frequencies they reproduce and how cleanly they handle them.

Can a bass test damage my speakers?

Sustained loud bass, especially below 30 Hz, can harm speakers not built for it. Start at a low volume, raise it gradually and stop if you hear rattling or distortion.

Why can't I hear very low frequencies?

Most small speakers and earbuds cannot reproduce much below 40 to 60 Hz, and human hearing fades below about 30 Hz. Larger drivers, a subwoofer or sealed over-ear headphones go lower.

What is a good lowest frequency for headphones?

Premium headphones usually reach about 20 Hz, budget earbuds stop around 40 to 60 Hz, and clean output at 30 Hz or below counts as excellent bass response.

Should I feel bass or hear it?

Below about 40 Hz bass is often felt as vibration more than heard, which is normal and part of the experience in music, movies and games.

How do I test my subwoofer?

Sweep the tone from 20 Hz to 80 Hz and listen for clean, even output. Rattling points to loose parts and a sudden volume drop shows roll-off.
